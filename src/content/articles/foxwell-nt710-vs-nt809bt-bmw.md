Buy the **NT710** if BMW ECU coding is part of the job and the BMW is effectively the only make you will scan. Buy the **NT809BT** if one tool has to cover a mixed fleet and you can work without ECU coding.

Foxwell publishes both tools in a single [buyer guide](https://www.foxwelldiag.com/pages/buyer-guide) matrix, which makes this an unusually clean comparison: one vendor, one table, no reconciliation between competing marketing pages. That table separates the two on exactly two rows. Advanced coding is listed for the NT710 and not for the NT809BT. Updates are lifetime on the NT710 and three years on the NT809BT.

Everything else people argue about, screen size, processor, battery, is secondary. Both run Android 9.0, both carry 32GB of storage, both are bidirectional, and neither is an ECU programming tool.

## The two rows that actually separate them

Foxwell's buyer guide lists advanced ECU coding for the NT710, GT60, NT1009 and NT909, each qualified "(for specific models)". The NT809BT is not on that list. The same guide puts the NT710 in the lifetime free update group and gives the NT809BT a three year term.

Those two facts point in opposite directions, and that is the whole decision. The NT710 is a single-make tool that goes deeper on one brand and keeps updating indefinitely. The NT809BT is a multi-brand tool that goes wider across a fleet and stops updating on a clock.

## BMW ECU coding is the one-way decision

The NT710 page describes "Exclusive ECU Coding fit for BMW E/F/G Chassis" and personalisation including hidden feature activation, immediately qualified with "This features are only supported on select models". Foxwell states the coverage boundary itself; it does not claim every function on every BMW.

Two cautions matter before you treat that as settled. First, Foxwell's own NT710 material is not internally consistent about which BMW generations the coding claim covers, and we worked that contradiction through in the [NT710 versus Autel MK900 comparison](/guides/foxwell-nt710-vs-autel-mk900-bmw/). Treat G chassis coding as unconfirmed until Foxwell verifies it against your VIN in writing.

Second, do not read the NT809BT's function list as a quiet coding claim. It includes "Injector Coding", which Foxwell describes as writing or updating injector codes in the ECU after replacing injectors. That is a service routine inside a defined procedure. It is not module personalisation, and it does not make the NT809BT a substitute for a coding tool. The distinction between a reset routine and genuine coding is the same one covered in [BMW coding vs programming](/guides/bmw-coding-vs-programming/).

Neither tool is claimed to flash or reprogram a control module.

## The update clock: lifetime against three years

Foxwell's lifetime language for the NT710 is explicit: "Get lifetime software updates at no extra cost, with no subscription or renewal fees". The NT809BT is documented at three years of Wi-Fi updates.

For a BMW owner keeping a car for a decade, that gap is the largest ownership difference between these two tools, and it runs the opposite way to the hardware. The cheaper-feeling single-make handheld keeps receiving software indefinitely. The larger multi-brand tablet has a documented end to its update term. Foxwell does not publish a renewal price for the NT809BT after year three, so treat post-term updates as an unpriced unknown and confirm it with Foxwell before buying on a long horizon.

## Corded handheld against wireless VCI

The NT710 connects by cable and is powered through the OBD port. Its Wi-Fi module exists for over-the-air software updates, not for vehicle communication. The NT809BT uses a Bluetooth VCI that stays in the OBD socket while the tablet moves around the car.

This decides more about the work than the spec sheet suggests. Anything requiring you to sit in the driver's seat, operate a switch, or watch a component while reading live data is easier with a wireless VCI. Anything done leaning into the footwell for a few minutes is unaffected. A cable also removes pairing as a failure mode, which is worth something on a tool you use twice a year.

## Adding a second make to the NT710

The NT710 ships with one vehicle make chosen at purchase. Foxwell sells [extra brand software](https://www.foxwelldiag.com/products/extra-vehicle-software) at $80.00 per package, and BMW, MINI and Rolls-Royce are grouped into a single package, so a BMW and a MINI in the same household cost one addition, not two. Foxwell states that the software "is a virtual product and cannot be refunded once it is authorized" against your serial number.

That non-refundable, serial-locked model is the thing to price honestly. One BMW plus one unrelated make is the tool plus $80. Three unrelated makes starts to approach the multi-brand tool you did not buy. The cross-brand version of that arithmetic is worked through in the [NT710 versus Autel MK900 comparison](/guides/foxwell-nt710-vs-autel-mk900-bmw/).

## Which one belongs on your BMW

Choose the **NT710** when the BMW or MINI is the car you are buying the tool for, when you want coding and personalisation on the list, and when you expect to keep the tool long enough for lifetime updates to matter. Confirm your exact chassis against Foxwell's coverage check first, and get the G chassis coding question answered in writing if your car is current generation.

Choose the **NT809BT** when the household or workshop spans several makes, when a wireless VCI genuinely changes how you work, and when full-system diagnostics with bidirectional tests and service resets is the actual requirement. Accept that ECU coding is not on its documented list and that the update term ends.
